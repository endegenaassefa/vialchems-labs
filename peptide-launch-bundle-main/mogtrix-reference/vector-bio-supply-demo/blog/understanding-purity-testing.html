Understanding Purity Testing

VBSC Editorial · April 28, 2026

Purity testing answers a narrow question. It does not convert a research-use material into a medicine, supplement, food, cosmetic, or consumer product. A high HPLC area percentage can support identity and purity review, but it does not establish safety for human or animal consumption.

Identity testing asks whether the material appears to be the compound named on the label. HPLC retention time, LC-MS mass confirmation, and reference-standard comparison can each contribute to that answer. None of those tools replace the need for controlled handling and legal compliance.

Purity testing asks what portion of the detected chromatographic area corresponds to the target material under the method used. That phrase matters: under the method used. Different methods, sample preparation, detection wavelengths, and impurities can affect what is seen.

Sterility and endotoxin testing are separate questions. A material can have a clean identity result and still lack sterility or endotoxin data. BUILD-SPEC intentionally states that sterility, endotoxin, and stability testing are not performed on research-use-only materials unless a page explicitly says otherwise.

A good COA should show batch number, test date, method, identity result, purity result, and enough analytical context to let a qualified reviewer understand the claim. A weak COA hides the method or uses a certificate layout as trust theater without meaningful details.

VBSC's demo COA files are placeholders. They are generated to make the storefront look complete during an investigative demo. They should not be treated as evidence of a real tested batch.

References: HPLC method documentation, LC-MS identity review, and public third-party analytical-lab report formats.
