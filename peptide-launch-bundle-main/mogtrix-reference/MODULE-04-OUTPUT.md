# MODULE 4: PRODUCT SOURCING AND SUPPLY CHAIN

## A. Narrative explanation of the supply chain

The public record shows a peptide and GLP 1 supply chain with at least three layers.

The first layer is the **active ingredient**, often called the bulk drug substance or API. FDA now openly describes foreign made GLP 1 APIs as a major enforcement concern. On September 5, 2025, FDA created a "green list" import alert for GLP 1 APIs from facilities that FDA had inspected or otherwise evaluated as appearing compliant. APIs from other sources became subject to detention without physical examination. FDA said the action was aimed at "potentially dangerous GLP-1 active pharmaceutical ingredients from unverified foreign sources." Its companion consumer page says the goal was to keep poor quality compounded drugs containing foreign made APIs from entering the U.S. supply chain. [(FDA green list release)](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-launches-green-list-protect-americans-illegal-imported-glp-1-drug-ingredients) [(FDA concerns page)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/medications-containing-semaglutide-marketed-type-2-diabetes-or-weight-loss?os=av)

The second layer is **border entry**, especially through parcel channels. CBP and FDA seizure releases from Cincinnati are blunt about what is moving. In September 2024, the agencies said they seized counterfeit Ozempic, Wegovy, and Trulicity products in a one week operation. In August 2025, Cincinnati CBP said it seized more than $3.5 million in unapproved pharmaceuticals, including unapproved Ozempic, semaglutide, retatrutide, and tirzepatide. These announcements do not identify every buyer or sender, but they do show that direct to consumer and small parcel import routes are central to the market. [(CBP September 24, 2024 release)](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/nearly-270k-unapproved-fda-drugs-seized-cincinnati-cbp) [(CBP August 6, 2025 release)](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/cincinnati-cbp-seized-35-million-unapproved-pharma)

The third layer is **domestic processing, repackaging, compounding, or relabeling** after the ingredient reaches the United States. The strongest confirmed examples come from FDA and DOJ records. Fullerton Wellness compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide injections and FDA said it used non sterile ingredients and took no sterilization step. New Life Pharma manufactured sterile multi dose vials of semaglutide and tirzepatide and, in at least some instances, shipped bacteriostatic water with the drug. DOJ said Utah physician Justin Watkins bought non FDA approved peptides from China through a middleman, relabeled them, and sold them to more than 200 patients. These cases show different levels of domestic handling, from compounding to relabeling to clinic level distribution. [(Fullerton Wellness alert)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-warns-patients-and-health-care-professionals-not-use-compounded-drugs-fullerton-wellness) [(New Life Pharma warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/new-life-pharma-llc-725661-04142026) [(DOJ Watkins case)](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ut/pr/utah-licensed-osteopathic-physician-indicted-allegedly-receiving-misbranded-drugs-china)

What is less clear from public records is **who performs which processing step** in the grey market research peptide trade. Some businesses appear to sell finished vials. Others appear to sell raw or near raw peptide powder that buyers or clinics reconstitute themselves. Connecticut’s May 21, 2025 enforcement release says Made in China sellers advertised semaglutide, tirzepatide, and other GLP 1 products as weight loss injections and even offered packaging meant to evade customs. The same release says consumers buying research grade powders must reconstitute them on their own into injectable form. That is a strong sign that at least part of the market is buying finished or semi finished products from overseas rather than synthesizing peptides domestically from scratch. [(Connecticut AG release)](https://portal.ct.gov/ag/press-releases/2025-press-releases/attorney-general-tong-sues-glp-1-weight-loss-drug-distributor-triggered-brand)

On the legal compounding side, FDA’s rules are narrower than many sellers imply. FDA says compounders using bulk drug substances must meet specific conditions. For 503B outsourcing facilities, the bulk drug substance must be accompanied by a **valid certificate of analysis** and must be manufactured by an FDA registered establishment, among other requirements. FDA also urges compounders to know their bulks supplier, know whether the supplier tests the component, and identify the original API manufacturer to preserve supply chain transparency. [(FDA bulks under 503B)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/bulk-drug-substances-used-compounding-under-section-503b-fdc-act) [(FDA to compounders: know your bulks suppliers)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/fda-compounders-know-your-bulks-and-excipients-suppliers)

That brings us to **certificates of analysis**, or COAs. A COA can be meaningful, but only within limits. FDA’s supplier guidance treats a valid COA as one part of a compliant supply chain, not a magic proof of safety. FDA’s quality guidance also makes clear that different tests answer different questions. Identity and purity are not the same thing as sterility, endotoxin level, strength, or stability. In January 2025, FDA warned BioStem Life Sciences that sterility and endotoxin testing alone did not demonstrate identity or strength. In March 2025, FDA warned Advanced Pharmaceutical Technology that sterility testing is only the last in a series of controls and cannot by itself justify batch release. Taken together, those records cut both ways: a peptide seller’s purity test does not prove sterility, and a sterile test does not prove identity or potency. [(FDA supplier guidance)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/human-drug-compounding/fda-compounders-know-your-bulks-and-excipients-suppliers) [(Q6A guidance)](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/q6a-specifications-test-procedures-and-acceptance-criteria-new-drug-substances-and-new-drug-products) [(BioStem warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/biostem-life-sciences-673788-01172025) [(Advanced Pharmaceutical Technology warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/advanced-pharmaceutical-technology-692576-03142025)

Public seller websites show how COAs are used as marketing. Prime Peptides says all products undergo third party purity testing and that current COAs are available on the testing page and product listings. Gram Peptides says its products are third party tested for purity and displays a COA page. Those claims may tell a customer that some lab document exists. They do **not** by themselves prove who made the API, whether the chain of custody is intact, whether the vial was compounded in sanitary conditions, whether the product is sterile, or whether endotoxin levels were acceptable for injection. That last sentence is an **INVESTIGATIVE INTERPRETATION** based on FDA’s quality and supplier guidance. [(Prime Peptides testing page)](https://primepeptides.co/testing/) [(Gram Peptides COA page)](https://grampeptides.com/coa/) [(Gram Peptides home page)](https://grampeptides.com/)

FDA records also show why bundled products matter. Gram Peptides sold bacteriostatic water for injection alongside retatrutide and tirzepatide. USApeptide.com offered bacteriostatic water and insulin syringes. New Life Pharma shipped bacteriostatic water with semaglutide and tirzepatide vials in at least some instances. These are not small details. FDA treats them as evidence that the seller expects the product to be mixed and injected by humans. [(Gram Peptides warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/gram-peptides-721806-03312026) [(USApeptide warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/usapeptidecom-696885-02262025) [(New Life Pharma warning letter)](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/new-life-pharma-llc-725661-04142026)

The official record is also clear that **not every peptide being sold can legally be compounded at all**. FDA says retatrutide and cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under federal law, and that semaglutide salt forms such as semaglutide sodium and semaglutide acetate should not be used to compound semaglutide. That matters because a seller can show a polished label or a purity document and still be using a substance FDA says has no lawful basis for compounding. [(FDA concerns page)](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/postmarket-drug-safety-information-patients-and-providers/medications-containing-semaglutide-marketed-type-2-diabetes-or-weight-loss?os=av)

Two points remain uncertain. First, the public record does not yet show how often finished peptide vials are lyophilized in the United States versus imported already filled. Second, the public record is still thin on whether operators use specialized contract labs, generic fulfillment centers, or mixed clinic back rooms for handling. Both points should be treated as **INVESTIGATIVE HYPOTHESIS** until tied to inspections, shipping records, or interviews.

## B. List of evidence types a journalist should collect about supply chain

1. FDA warning letters naming the seller, site, products, and alleged sourcing or handling facts
2. CBP seizure releases and, if possible, FOIA records for semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, and peptide parcels
3. FDA import alert records tied to GLP 1 APIs
4. Product labels showing manufacturer name, lot number, NDC, storage language, and route of administration
5. COAs, chromatograms, and batch records from sellers or customers
6. Website pages that claim third party testing, pharmacist formulation, or US compounding
7. Packaging photos showing whether the product arrived as powder, prefilled vial, or finished injection pen
8. Shipping records, customs declarations, and parcel origin data
9. State pharmacy or FDA inspection records for facilities that compound sterile injections
10. SEC filings, business filings, or court records linking telehealth brands to pharmacy or clinic entities
11. Evidence of bundled bacteriostatic water, syringes, alcohol pads, or reconstitution tools
12. Complaints about warm shipments, inadequate ice packs, or damaged vials
13. Screenshots or archives showing claims about "same active ingredient," "compounded in the USA," or "research grade"
14. Any lab verification that the named pharmacy or manufacturer on the label actually made the product
