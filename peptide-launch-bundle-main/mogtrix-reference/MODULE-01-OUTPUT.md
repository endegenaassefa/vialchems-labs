# MODULE 1: ENFORCEMENT ACTIONS DATABASE

Research window: January 1, 2024 through April 25, 2026

Method note: This is a first pass enforcement database built from official FDA, FTC, DOJ, CBP, state attorney general, and state pharmacy regulator records. I included confirmed actions I could document directly. Where FDA announced a larger wave of letters but did not name every target in the press release, I note that gap clearly.

## A. Numbered list of enforcement actions found

1. **US Chem Labs**
   1. Date: February 7, 2024
   2. Agency: FDA, CDER
   3. Products involved: Semaglutide, tirzepatide, thymalin
   4. What the company allegedly did: FDA said the site sold semaglutide and tirzepatide as "research chemicals only" while pairing them with claims about weight loss, diabetes, and other human effects. FDA said that made the products unapproved new drugs and misbranded drugs sold over the internet.
   5. Law cited: FD&C Act sections 301(a), 301(d), 502, and 505(a)
   6. What happened after: FDA demanded a written response within 15 working days. I did not locate a public closeout letter in this pass.
   7. Source: [FDA warning letter](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/us-chem-labs-669074-02072024)

2. **Synthetix Inc. dba Helix Chemical Supply**
   1. Date: February 7, 2024
   2. Agency: FDA, CDER
   3. Products involved: Semaglutide, tirzepatide
   4. What the company allegedly did: FDA said Helix sold semaglutide and tirzepatide for "research purposes" but used claims tied to Alzheimer’s disease, blood sugar, and insulin secretion. FDA treated those claims as evidence of intended human drug use.
   5. Law cited: FD&C Act sections 301(a), 301(d), 502(f)(1), and 505(a)
   6. What happened after: FDA demanded a written response within 15 working days. I did not locate a public closeout letter in this pass.
   7. Source: [FDA warning letter](https://www.fda.gov/inspections-compliance-enforcement-and-criminal-investigations/warning-letters/synthetix-inc-dba-helix-chemical-supply-668918-02072024)

3. **Isis Navarro Reyes, TikTok seller**
   1. Date: May 1, 2024
   2. Agency: DOJ, SDNY, with FDA OCI
   3. Products involved: Ozempic, Mesofrance, Axcion, other weight loss drugs
   4. What the company allegedly did: DOJ said Reyes marketed and sold prescription weight loss drugs through TikTok and encrypted messaging without a license and without prescriptions. Prosecutors said one buyer developed a mycobacterium abscessus infection and that a tested vial was positive for the bacterium.
   5. Law cited: Receipt and delivery of misbranded drugs, dispensing prescription drugs without prescriptions, conspiracy to introduce misbranded drugs into interstate commerce, and smuggling
   6. What happened after: Reyes was arrested and presented in federal court on May 1, 2024.
   7. Source: [DOJ press release](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/long-island-woman-arrested-selling-misbranded-and-adulterated-weight-loss-drugs)

4. **Counterfeit and unapproved weight loss drugs seized in Cincinnati**
   1. Date: September 24, 2024
   2. Agency: CBP and FDA
   3. Products involved: Counterfeit Ozempic, Wegovy, Trulicity, other unapproved drugs
   4. What the company allegedly did: This was not aimed at one named seller. CBP said a one week joint operation intercepted 63 shipments, including 12 shipments of counterfeit prefilled pens tied to high demand weight loss drugs.
   5. Law cited: FDA compliance authorities and CBP seizure authority for counterfeit and unapproved drugs
   6. What happened after: The shipments were seized. CBP publicized the operation as a border enforcement action.
   7. Source: [CBP release](https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/local-media-release/nearly-270k-unapproved-fda-drugs-seized-cincinnati-cbp)

5. **Fullerton Wellness LLC**

   5. Law cited: FDA safety authority over adulterated and unsafe drugs, tied to sterile compounding requirements
   6. What happened after: FDA said Fullerton Wellness voluntarily ceased operations after the state inspection.
   7. Source: [FDA drug safety alert](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/drug-alerts-and-statements/fda-warns-patients-and-health-care-professionals-not-use-compounded-drugs-fullerton-wellness)

## C. Summary of patterns

1. **FDA’s intended use theory is the backbone.** The agency repeatedly treated "research use only" language as meaningless when websites also used weight loss, blood sugar, appetite, insulin, or disease language. The warning letters to US Chem Labs, Helix, Prime Peptides, USApeptide, and Gram Peptides all show this.

2. **GLP 1 demand widened the enforcement field.** Early 2024 FDA letters focused on classic research peptide sellers. By late 2025 and early 2026, the crackdown expanded to telehealth style sites, med spas, and compounded GLP 1 marketers using branding that implied sameness with Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, or Zepbound.

3. **Injection risk keeps appearing.** FDA repeatedly stressed that injectable products carry higher public health risk because sterility failures and contamination can cause serious harm. That concern appears in Fullerton Wellness, USApeptide, Gram Peptides, and the counterfeit Ozempic alerts.

4. **Border enforcement is a major piece of the story.** CBP and FDA publicized repeated parcel seizures in Cincinnati, and FDA created a GLP 1 API import alert in September 2025. Connecticut’s case against Made in China also points toward customs evasion as part of the supply chain story.

5. **Social media and telehealth marketing are now enforcement evidence.** Prime Peptides shows FDA citing Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube. Reyes shows a criminal case built around TikTok. NextMed shows FTC targeting fake reviews, hidden fees, and manipulated before and after claims.

6. **There is a split between warning stage enforcement and criminal cases.** FDA often starts with warning letters. DOJ cases appear when there is direct patient treatment, relabeling, concealment, or serious harm allegations. FTC and state AGs focus more on deceptive advertising and consumer protection.

## E. Gaps

1. FDA announced a March 3, 2026 batch of 30 telehealth warning letters, but the press release did not name all of the targets. A follow up search should try to build the full roster from the warning letter index and archived press materials.

2. State pharmacy board actions are scattered and hard to search in one place. Washington was easy to document. Other states likely have more actions buried in board dockets, minutes, or consent orders.

3. CBP parcel seizure data is public only in fragments. The agency publicizes selected operations, but not a complete public list of semaglutide, tirzepatide, retatrutide, or peptide parcel seizures.

4. Closeout and compliance outcomes are inconsistent. FDA warning letter pages often do not show whether a company shut down, rebranded, or quietly kept selling.

5. Some criminal matters involve clinics and practitioners rather than pure ecommerce sites. Those still matter because they show how grey market peptide products move from online sourcing into patient treatment.

6. This first pass is strongest on federal actions. State AG, pharmacy board, and customs records need a second pass.
