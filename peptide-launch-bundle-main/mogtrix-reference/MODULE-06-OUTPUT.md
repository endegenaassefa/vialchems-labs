# MODULE 6: PAYMENT PROCESSING AND MONEY FLOW

## A. Narrative explanation of the payment ecosystem

The first fact to understand is that mainstream payment processors and card networks do not like businesses that look illegal, deceptive, or too likely to generate chargebacks. That matters here because grey market peptide sellers often sit in exactly that risk zone.

Official policy language makes the basic problem clear. Stripe says its services cannot be used for illegal activity or for products that violate law in the jurisdictions targeted by the business. PayPal’s acceptable use materials list prescription drugs and illegal narcotics as prohibited categories. Square’s payment terms go even further for remote commerce and say sellers may not use Square for internet, mail order, or telephone order pharmacies or pharmacy referral services where medication is fulfilled after an internet or telephone consultation without a physical visit. [(Stripe restricted businesses)](https://stripe.com/en-mx/restricted-businesses) [(PayPal acceptable use help page)](https://www.paypal.com/fk/cshelp/article/what-is-paypal%E2%80%99s-acceptable-use-policy-and-where-can-i-find-it-help270) [(Square payment terms)](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5089-prohibited-goods-and-services-with-square-point-of-sale)

That does not mean every peptide site disappears from card processing. It means they often have to work through more fragile or more indirect setups. Xcel Peptides provides a good current example. Its own site has a page explaining "How pay with Credit/Debit card via MAX REDEMPTION" and says most research peptide companies tend to get their transactions blocked by bank accounts, so it is best to use a third party to make that payment. Xcel’s terms page also says the website is operated by Xcel Research LLC and that payments are made payable to Xcel Research LLC. That is useful evidence because it shows both the legal payee and the presence of a separate payment layer. [(Xcel Max Redemption page)](https://www.xcelpeptides.com/max-redemption-info/) [(Xcel terms)](https://www.xcelpeptides.com/terms-of-service/)

OnePeptideShop shows a more ordinary card style setup on paper. Its terms say it accepts Visa, American Express, Mastercard, Discover, and PayPal. On its face, that looks mainstream. But the same terms also identify the operator as EA WORKS Limited Liability Company, a Wyoming entity that public records link to other peptide retail domains. For reporting purposes, that means the payment trail should be checked against the visible brand, the legal entity in the terms, and any descriptor that appears on a bank or card statement. [(OnePeptideShop terms)](https://onepeptideshop.com/terms-and-conditions/) [(Wyoming EA WORKS business record)](https://wyobiz.wyo.gov/Business/FilingDetails.aspx?eFNum=040026109250153238100098184023250152046009201137)

The broader enforcement record suggests three recurring money flow problems.

First, **merchant instability**. If a processor decides the seller’s activity is prohibited or too risky, the seller may lose card access mid operation. That helps explain why some sellers emphasize backup payment paths, third party platforms, or rapid domain changes. This sentence is an **INVESTIGATIVE INTERPRETATION** based on processor policies, Xcel’s public payment page, and the repeated use of multiple domains and entities documented in Module 3. [(Xcel Max Redemption page)](https://www.xcelpeptides.com/max-redemption-info/) [(Stripe restricted businesses)](https://stripe.com/en-mx/restricted-businesses) [(Square payment terms)](https://squareup.com/help/us/en/article/5089-prohibited-goods-and-services-with-square-point-of-sale)

Second, **descriptor confusion**. The consumer may buy from a storefront brand but the legal entity in the terms may be different. That means the card statement, refund record, or dispute notice may point to an LLC name the buyer never noticed. Xcel’s site and OnePeptideShop’s terms both show this split in public. [(Xcel terms)](https://www.xcelpeptides.com/terms-of-service/) [(OnePeptideShop terms)](https://onepeptideshop.com/terms-and-conditions/)

Third, **price opacity and hidden commitments**. FTC’s NextMed complaint is not about research peptides sold as raw chemicals. But it is highly relevant to the broader GLP 1 money story because it shows the kind of financial practices regulators are already targeting in this market: advertised low monthly prices, extra drug and lab costs, annual commitment traps, early termination fees, and fake or manipulated reviews used to support conversion. [(FTC NextMed release)](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/07/ftc-takes-action-against-telemedicine-firm-nextmed-over-charges-it-used-misleading-prices-fake)

On **alternative payment methods**, the public record in this pass is thinner than the folklore around the market. I did not directly confirm widespread use of ACH, eCheck, wire, cryptocurrency, Zelle, Cash App, Venmo, money orders, or prepaid cards from official records for the named companies in Modules 1 through 4. Those channels are commonly discussed by consumers and vendors in the wider online peptide world, but in this reporting memo they should be treated as **INVESTIGATIVE HYPOTHESIS** unless documented through screenshots, receipts, checkout pages, or customer statements. The one confirmed adaptation in this pass is Xcel’s use of the Max Redemption card flow. [(Xcel Max Redemption page)](https://www.xcelpeptides.com/max-redemption-info/)

The safest conclusion is narrow. Grey market peptide payment systems appear to rely on a mix of ordinary ecommerce checkout tools, legal entities that do not match the public brand, and backup routing when mainstream processors view the activity as too risky.

## B. Money flow description

1. Customer lands on a brand site and chooses a peptide or GLP 1 product.
2. Customer proceeds to checkout under a storefront brand name.
3. Payment is routed either to the named operating entity in the site terms or through a third party payment layer. This step is confirmed for Xcel and OnePeptideShop.
4. The merchant of record or related entity receives settlement from the processor or intermediary.
5. Funds are used to cover shipping, platform fees, refunds, marketing, and inventory purchases.
6. Upstream funds may then move to a supplier, repackager, clinic, telehealth contractor, or pharmacy depending on the model. This step is an **INVESTIGATIVE HYPOTHESIS** for most named sellers because the public record rarely shows the full ledger.
7. If the processor freezes or terminates the account, the seller may have to move to a new payment path, brand, or domain. This is an **INVESTIGATIVE INTERPRETATION** supported indirectly by processor rules and entity splitting evidence.

## C. List of evidence a journalist should collect about payments

1. Screenshots of checkout pages and payment options
2. Terms pages naming the payee entity
3. Card or bank statement descriptors from willing customers
4. Refund confirmation emails and the sender entity
5. Chargeback notices or merchant dispute emails
6. Processor logos and plugin names visible in page source
7. Alternative payment instructions sent after checkout, if any
8. Subscription terms, cancellation terms, and early termination fees
9. State business records for every payee entity named in terms or receipts
10. SEC filings or lawsuits that mention payment disputes, reserves, or account freezes
11. Complaints describing unexpected billing or hard to cancel memberships
