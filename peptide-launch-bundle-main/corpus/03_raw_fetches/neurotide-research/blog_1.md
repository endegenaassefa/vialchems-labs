It has been detected in the brain, hypothalamus, and peripheral tissues, as well as in biological fluids such as plasma and cerebrospinal fluid.

It is studied for its potential interaction with sleep-related brain activity and regulatory pathways.

DSIP has been studied in relation to slow-wave (deep) sleep.

Research has explored whether it may influence sleep patterns, particularly in models with disrupted sleep.

Unlike traditional sedative compounds, DSIP is studied as a potential modulator of sleep processes rather than a direct inducer.

## Stress and Neuroendocrine Research

DSIP has been studied for its interaction with stress-related pathways, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

Research has explored its relationship with hormonal signaling and stress response regulation.

## Additional Research Areas

Preclinical studies have explored DSIP in several areas:

  * Neuroendocrine signaling pathways
  * Stress-related biological processes
  * Neurological models in animal studies
  * Sleep and recovery-related mechanisms



These findings are based on early-stage and preclinical research.

## What Is Still Unknown?

Despite ongoing interest, several aspects of DSIP remain unclear:

  * No confirmed dedicated receptor has been identified
  * Results across studies are inconsistent
  * Long-term human data is limited



These limitations are why DSIP remains a research compound.

## Why Researchers Study DSIP

DSIP continues to be studied due to its unique characteristics:

## [How to Store Research Peptides Without Losing Potency](https://neurotideresearch.com/how-to-store-research-peptides/)

**Peptide storage** is a critical factor in maintaining the stability and integrity of research compounds. Improper storage can lead to degradation, oxidation, or loss of activity, directly impacting research results.

This guide explains how to properly store peptides based on their form and common laboratory practices.

## Why Peptide Storage Matters

Peptides are sensitive to environmental factors such as heat, light, moisture, and oxygen.

Exposure to these conditions can alter their structure and reduce reliability in research applications.

## Lyophilized vs Reconstituted Peptides

### Lyophilized Peptides

Lyophilized (freeze-dried) peptides are more stable and preferred for long-term storage.

This form reduces exposure to moisture and helps preserve structural integrity.

### Reconstituted Peptides

Once dissolved in a solvent, peptide stability decreases.

Reconstituted solutions should be stored properly and used within a limited timeframe.

Learn more in our [peptide reconstitution guide](https://neurotideresearch.com/how-to-reconstitute-peptides/).

## Temperature Guidelines

### Room Temperature (15–25°C)

Suitable for short-term storage of lyophilized peptides only.

Not recommended for long-term storage.

### Refrigeration (2–8°C)

Standard storage condition for both short-term lyophilized peptides and reconstituted solutions.

### Freezing (-20°C)

Recommended for long-term storage of lyophilized peptides.

Allow vials to reach room temperature before opening to prevent moisture exposure.

### Ultra-Low Temperature (-80°C)

Used for highly sensitive compounds or long-term storage in advanced research settings.

## Peptide-Specific Considerations

### pH-Sensitive Peptides

Some peptides require specific solvents to maintain stability.

This is important for compounds such as [Semaglutide](https://neurotideresearch.com/semaglutide-research-overview/) and [Tirzepatide](https://neurotideresearch.com/tirzepatide-research-overview/).

### Oxidation-Sensitive Peptides

Peptides containing certain amino acids may degrade when exposed to oxygen.

Proper sealing and minimal air exposure are important.

### Disulfide Bond Peptides

These peptides require careful handling to maintain structural integrity.

## Reconstitution Best Practices

Proper technique helps maintain peptide quality:

  * Allow vial to reach room temperature before opening
  * Add solvent slowly along the vial wall
  * Gently swirl — do not shake
  * Avoid contamination



See our [full reconstitution guide](https://neurotideresearch.com/how-to-reconstitute-peptides/) for details.

## Light and Moisture Exposure

Peptides should be stored in dark, sealed containers.

Light exposure and humidity can accelerate degradation.

## Freeze-Thaw Cycles

Repeated freezing and thawing can damage peptide structure.

To avoid this, researchers often divide solutions into smaller portions before storage.

## [FDA Peptide Reclassification 2026: What You Need to Know](https://neurotideresearch.com/fda-peptide-reclassification-2026/)

## What Is the FDA Peptide Reclassification?

On February 27, 2026, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. announced during an appearance on The Joe Rogan Experience (Episode #2461) that approximately 14 of the 19 peptides placed on the FDA’s Category 2 restricted list in 2023 would be moved back to Category 1 status. If formalized, this would restore the ability of licensed compounding pharmacies to prepare these peptides under physician prescription.

As of the time of writing, the FDA has not yet published its formal updated list. The announcement signals a significant shift in policy, but the official regulatory action is still pending publication.

## Background: What Happened in 2023?

In late 2023, the FDA reclassified 19 widely used peptides as Category 2 bulk drug substances under Section 503A of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Category 2 designation is reserved for compounds the agency identifies as presenting significant safety risks, effectively prohibiting licensed compounding pharmacies from preparing them.

The FDA’s stated rationale for the 2023 restrictions centered on concerns about immunogenicity, manufacturing impurities, and insufficient large-scale human clinical trial data for the affected compounds.

The full list of peptides placed on the Category 2 restricted list in 2023 included:

  * BPC-157
  * Thymosin Alpha-1
  * Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment (TB-500)
  * AOD-9604
  * CJC-1295
  * Ipamorelin Acetate
  * Selank Acetate
  * Semax
  * KPV
  * GHK-Cu
  * MOTS-C
  * Epitalon
  * Kisspeptin
  * DSIP (Delta Sleep Inducing Peptide)
  * Melanotan I
  * Melanotan II
  * GHRP-2
  * GHRP-6
  * LL-37



## What Is Category 1 vs. Category 2?

Under the FDA’s bulk drug substances framework for 503A compounding pharmacies:

  * **Category 1:** Bulk drug substances under evaluation with sufficient supporting safety information. Compounding pharmacies may prepare these substances while they remain under FDA review, provided a valid patient-specific prescription exists.
  * **Category 2:** Bulk drug substances identified as presenting significant safety risks. These cannot be used in compounding unless the FDA formally authorizes their use through rulemaking.



It is important to note that Category 1 status is not the same as FDA drug approval. FDA approval requires completion of Phase I through Phase III clinical trials and a formal New Drug Application — a separate and more extensive regulatory process.

## The 2026 Announcement: Which Peptides Are Expected to Return?

Based on Kennedy’s February 27, 2026 announcement and reporting from multiple regulatory analysts, the following peptides are expected to move from Category 2 back to Category 1. This is not an official FDA list — the formal publication has not yet been released as of the time of writing.

Peptides expected to return to Category 1 status include:

  * **BPC-157** — studied in preclinical models for tissue repair and gastrointestinal research
  * **Thymosin Alpha-1** — an immune-modulating peptide approved as a pharmaceutical in over 30 countries outside the United States
  * **TB-500 (Thymosin Beta-4 Fragment)** — studied for cell migration and wound healing in preclinical models
  * **AOD-9604** — studied in research models related to fat metabolism
  * **CJC-1295** — a growth hormone releasing hormone analog (status disputed between sources — some analysts list it as returning, others flag it as potentially remaining restricted)
  * **Ipamorelin** — a selective growth hormone secretagogue studied in preclinical and some clinical contexts
  * **Selank** — a synthetic neuropeptide studied for anxiolytic and cognitive effects in preclinical models
  * **Semax** — a neuropeptide studied for cognitive and neuroprotective effects, with clinical use in Russia
  * **KPV** — a tripeptide studied for anti-inflammatory properties in preclinical research
  * **GHK-Cu** — a copper-binding peptide studied for wound healing and gene expression modulation
  * **MOTS-C** — a mitochondria-derived peptide studied for metabolic regulation
  * **Epitalon** — a tetrapeptide studied for telomere-related research and longevity applications
  * **Kisspeptin** — a neuropeptide with active clinical trials studying reproductive hormone regulation
  * **DSIP** — a peptide studied in preclinical sleep and stress regulation research



## Which Peptides Are Expected to Remain Restricted?

Based on Kennedy’s statement that litigation prevented the reinstatement of all 19 peptides, and analysis from regulatory experts, approximately 5 peptides are expected to remain on the Category 2 restricted list:

  * **Melanotan II** — flagged due to concerns about melanocyte stimulation and potential cardiovascular effects
  * **GHRP-2** — noted for more complex side effect profiles including cortisol and prolactin elevation
  * **GHRP-6** — similar concerns to GHRP-2
  * **LL-37** — limited human safety data available
  * **PEG-MGF** — minimal published clinical evidence



## What Does This Mean for the Research Community?

For researchers and the broader research peptide community, several important distinctions apply:

**Reclassification is not FDA approval.** Category 1 status means licensed compounding pharmacies may legally prepare these substances under physician prescription while they remain under FDA review. It does not mean these compounds have completed the clinical trial process required for FDA drug approval, nor does it establish proven safety, efficacy, or standardized dosing for any indication.

**Research use vendors are not directly affected.** The reclassification changes the regulatory status for licensed 503A compounding pharmacies. Companies selling peptides labeled “for research use only” operate in a separate legal framework. The reclassification does not change the legal status of research-grade peptides.

**The formal FDA list has not been published.** As of the time of writing, the official updated Category 1 and Category 2 lists have not been released by the FDA. Kennedy’s announcement signals the direction of policy but does not constitute a regulatory change until formally published.

## Historical Context: Why Were These Peptides Restricted in 2023?

The 2023 restrictions generated significant discussion in the research and clinical communities. Critics of the original decision argued that the FDA lacked a specific safety signal sufficient to justify restriction for most of the affected compounds, and that the restrictions had the unintended consequence of driving demand toward unregulated gray-market sources.

Kennedy acknowledged this dynamic in his February 2026 announcement, stating that the Category 2 designations had created and accelerated the very gray market they were designed to prevent. This recognition formed part of the rationale for the proposed reclassification.

## What to Watch For

The research community should monitor the following developments:

  * Official FDA publication of the updated Category 1 and Category 2 bulk drug substances list
  * State-level pharmacy board announcements, as individual states may respond at different timelines
  * Any updates regarding CJC-1295, whose status remains disputed between regulatory analysts
  * The separate legislative push by supplement manufacturers to classify certain peptides as dietary supplement ingredients under DSHEA — a different regulatory pathway that could eventually allow over-the-counter access for some compounds
