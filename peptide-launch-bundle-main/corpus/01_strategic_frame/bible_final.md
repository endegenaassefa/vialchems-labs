*The strategic foundation document for the trial-run launch. This is Stage 2 of a five-stage chain. It feeds Stage 3 (Combined Context Doc), which feeds Stage 4 (Research Pipeline), which feeds Stage 5 (Unified Operating Report). What this document contains is shaped by that position. Operational specifics (site copy, opening SKU prices, channel budgets, verbatim disclaimers, day-by-day plans) are deliberately deferred to Stage 4 and Stage 5.*

*The trial run is one specific test: build a peptide e-commerce site under a throwaway brand, deploy it, and find the optimal way to advertise it. Everything in this Bible serves that test. Anything that creeps into "what happens after the trial succeeds" (the LA move, the full Mocktricks operation, the long-horizon revenue targets) is deliberately scoped out so the research AI optimizes for the actual question, which is how to advertise this specific website to this specific buyer at this specific moment.*

This is the most important forward-feeding section in the document. The downstream channel research is only as good as the buyer profile that drives it. Be specific or the rest of the pipeline shallows.

They are post-ironic, they are aware that peptides exist, they are not afraid of the gray legality, and they buy on cultural recognition more than on clinical credentials. Posture B (Jester Labs, Psycho Labs) is built for this audience.

Adjacent and partly overlapping: the biohacking community, which is older on average, more technical, more clinical-leaning, and the audience Posture A (NZT Peptides, Hunter Eyes Labs, Adam Labs) would target. Abby's read in the transcript is that this audience already has its preferred sources and is hard to win on price or quality alone, which is what made him lean Posture B in the live debate.

Further adjacent: the broader gym/fitness culture that buys SARMs, peptides, and TRT-related products through gray channels. This is the largest audience in absolute terms but the least culturally specific. It is best reached through paid social and search, less through community channels.

**Behavioral.** This buyer searches for peptide names directly ('BPC-157,' 'TB-500,' 'Retatrutide,' 'GHK-Cu'). Abby names a specific contact in the looksmaxxing space at 30K Instagram followers as a potential influencer touchpoint, which tells you the order-of-magnitude creator tier where the conversation actually happens.

**Price sensitivity.** This is a per-mg market. Buyers compare per-mg prices across vendors directly. The transcript does not specify the team's planned price point relative to competitors, which is a research-pipeline question (Slice 6). The throwaway brand has no reputation premium, which means it has to either price at or below the established-vendor median to win first orders, or compensate for the price gap with cultural alignment.

**The two simultaneous goals.** Reaching this buyer is a structurally hard problem because you have to satisfy two goals that pull in opposite directions:

The first goal is that the website, the brand language, and the marketing must not draw platform-policy, payment-processor, or regulatory attention. Meta, Google, Apple, Stripe, Shopify, and the FDA all have policies that, applied strictly, would shut down a peptide store on day one. Surviving requires careful framing ("research use only"), surface-level cleanliness, and avoiding any signal that the buyer is being marketed to as a consumer of human-use products.

The second goal is that the right buyer must still be able to find the website. Organic discovery requires forum presence, creator relationships, search visibility, and word-of-mouth. Paid discovery requires ads that the platforms will actually serve, which means the creative cannot read like a peptide ad to the platform's classifier.

These goals are in direct tension because the easiest way to satisfy goal one is to be invisible, and the easiest way to satisfy goal two is to be loud. The entire research pipeline downstream of this Bible exists to map the narrow corridor where both goals are satisfied at once: which exact channels, which exact creative angles, which exact compliance language, which exact platforms a small new vendor in this category can actually use without getting nuked. Until that corridor is mapped, you do not know what the trial-run launch should look like. That is why the Bible refuses to specify it.

## 9. The Compliance Posture Commitment

You have committed, in the transcript, to operating inside the legal gray zone the established research-chemical industry occupies, not outside of it. That commitment has specific contents.

- "For research use only."
- "Not for human consumption."
- An age gate of some kind on the site.

**What the team will not say:**
- Any medical claim about what the peptide does for a human user.
- Any dosing recommendation.
- Any therapeutic outcome claim.
- Any framing that markets to consumers rather than to "researchers."

**What the team has not yet decided (these go to Section 14):**
- The verbatim disclaimer wording across the home page, product pages, cart, checkout, and post-purchase emails.
- The age-gate placement and design (modal on first visit, persistent in checkout, both, neither).
- The COA hosting model (per-batch PDFs linked from product pages, on-request via email, none, third-party verifiable).
- The ID verification policy (none, soft KYC at checkout, hard KYC, jurisdiction-dependent).
- The jurisdiction restrictions list (which states and countries you will not ship to).
- The terms of service, privacy policy, and shipping policy specific to this category.
- The handling of a customer who emails asking dosing questions (auto-response, polite decline, escalation, ban).
- The handling of a takedown request from a payment processor or registrar.

These are not small items. The verbatim wording of the disclaimer is what determines whether the platform-policy classifier flags the site. The COA hosting model is what determines whether forum buyers will trust you on first sight. The research pipeline's compliance slice exists specifically to inventory the language and architecture that working vendors use, so you can converge on a posture instead of inventing one.

One non-trivial thing that surfaced in the transcript and needs an explicit position. Abby and Caleb floated the idea of LARPing reviews (faking reviews on the site to bootstrap trust). That is a posture call. Faking reviews on your own site is a permanent reputational liability the moment it is discovered, which in the source-review-thread culture of this category is not a question of if but of when. The Bible's position is straightforward: do not fake reviews. Run the trial run with no reviews on the site at launch and let the first ones accumulate organically, even if it slows trust-building. The research pipeline will surface what trust-signals high-trust new vendors actually use in their first 90 days (Section 14 captures this).

## 10. Trial Run Resourcing

The trial run is deliberately small. Its job is to produce a demand signal cheaply, before any meaningful capital is committed to the bigger plan.

What the team can know now: the trial-run total cost is meant to fit comfortably inside the available capital pool with reserve to spare, so that a failed test does not kill the team's optionality. What the team will know after the research pipeline lands: the realistic CAC range by channel, the per-mg price floor across competitors, the realistic stock buy required to fulfill orders fast enough to compete on shipping speed, and the realistic ad-test budget needed to produce statistically meaningful signal in 21-30 days.

**Capital pool sits behind the trial run.** The team has access to a larger capital pool (cash, available credit, additional contributions from founders) that can fund the bigger plan if the trial validates. That pool is not the trial-run budget. It is the reserve that gets deployed after the trial-run signal comes back positive. Treat the trial run as the gate; treat the capital pool as what flows through the gate if the gate opens.

**Trial-run timeline.**
1. Pre-launch (now to roughly day 7-14 from the recording): pick brand posture and name, register LLC for the throwaway entity, register domain, build the site, set up payment processor (the working option from Slice 2), write compliance copy (informed by Slice 5), set up tracking, prepare initial creative for the chosen brand posture, place opening stock order if needed.

## 13. The Hostile Read

You have a US-based source you describe as "completely tested and safe," but the recording does not establish that you have asked the source about MOQ, restocking lead time, lab-test passthrough, or what happens if the source goes dark. That is the single point of failure your trial run is built on, and you do not have a backup.

The trial run itself, scoped narrowly, is defensible. Build a site, run ads, see if a stranger orders. That is a real test. The risk is that the test produces ambiguous data because too many variables are uncontrolled (brand posture, channel mix, creative angle, price point, processor choice, compliance language are all moving simultaneously), and ambiguous data leads to wrong conclusions. The research pipeline is supposed to control most of those variables before you spend ad dollars, which is why running the pipeline cleanly before launch matters more than launching fast.

The strongest version of this critique is not that any single piece is wrong. The strongest version is that you are stacking three sources of risk (regulatory, payment, brand-trust) on a trial run with too many moving parts, and the throwaway frame is the team's way of pre-forgiving the failure mode rather than designing around it.

The defense, if you have one, is the wedge in Q4: this is a 21-day, lean, fully disposable test of a category your founder credibly believes he can read culturally. If the test produces a real demand signal, the rest of the bigger plan adapts. If it produces nothing, you have spent a small fraction of your available capital and a few weeks, not a year. That is a real defense. It depends on the test being run well, which depends on the research pipeline doing its job, which is why the pipeline exists.

## 15. The Decision Criteria

The research pipeline will produce a lot of data. Without explicit decision criteria, you will spin on the data instead of acting on it. These are the criteria, with weights where weights apply. Apply them to the research outputs as soon as they land.

**Brand Posture Decision (A vs B).**
1. Cost to first traction at trial-run budget (40%). Which posture's channel mix produces the cheapest path from $0 to first paid order from a stranger, given Slice 4 and Slice 6 outputs?
2. Platform and payment-processor risk profile (25%). Which posture is less likely to trigger ad classifier rejection, processor termination, or registrar takedown given Slice 2 and Slice 5 outputs?
3. Cultural fit with the buyer profile (20%). Which posture matches the audience the team can actually reach (i.e., the audience the founders' existing social presence overlaps with), given Slice 4 and Section 6?
4. Long-term defensibility if the brand is kept rather than thrown away (15%). Which posture is easier to scale into a permanent brand if the trial run succeeds beyond expectations?

**Channel Mix Decision (first 3 channels for launch).**
1. CAC at trial-run budget (45%). Which three channels have the lowest realistic CAC for this category at small budgets per Slice 4 and channel-cost benchmarks?
2. Speed to first signal (25%). Which three channels produce evaluable data within 21 days?
3. Compatibility with the chosen brand posture (20%). Channels favored by Posture A and Posture B differ; pick from inside the chosen posture.
4. Team capability to execute (10%). Channels the founders can actually run well given existing skills (organic clip content vs paid search vs forum-native engagement).

**Opening SKU Decision (5-10 SKUs at launch).**
1. Per-mg margin at the team's source price (35%). Pick from the highest-margin band per Slice 6.
2. Demand signal in the buyer profile (35%). Pick from the most-discussed peptides in Slice 3 and most-searched per Slice 4.
3. Supply availability and restocking reliability (20%). Pick from SKUs the source carries reliably with low MOQ.
4. Compliance simplicity (10%). Avoid SKUs in the most enforcement-active categories (e.g., GLP-1 analogues if Slice 5 surfaces FDA escalation).

**Pricing Decision (relative to the competitive matrix).**
1. Beat the median per-mg price by 5-10% on the top 3 SKUs (the loss-leaders for first-order conversion).
2. Match or beat the 25th percentile on the next 4-5 SKUs (the volume drivers).
3. Match the median on the rest (the catalog filler that signals selection without sacrificing margin).
4. Recompute weekly during the trial run if Slice 6 data shows competitor moves.

**Compliance Posture Decision.**
1. Adopt the verbatim disclaimer language used by the most-trusted Slice 1 vendor in the same posture as your chosen brand (modified only for legal review by an actual lawyer if Slice 5 surfaces a specific risk).
2. Adopt the COA model used by the most-trusted Slice 1 vendor in the same posture.
3. Adopt the jurisdictional restriction list of the strictest Slice 1 vendor (i.e., be at least as restrictive as the most cautious working vendor).
4. Do not fake reviews. Do not make medical claims. Do not market to "consumers." Do not handle dosing questions.

**Go / No-Go Decision at the end of the trial run.** The transcript does not specify go/no-go thresholds. These are the criteria the team should adopt unless Slice 6 plus channel benchmarks suggest different ones:

- **Strong go (commit to the bigger plan):** ≥30 orders from strangers in the trial-run window, repeat-buyer rate ≥15%, blended CAC under 50% of gross margin per order, organic forum mentions ≥3.
- **Weak go (continue, restructure):** 5-29 orders from strangers, any positive signal in any channel, blended CAC under 100% of gross margin per order. Iterate brand or channel mix; do not yet commit the bigger plan.
- **Pivot:** zero strangers ordering but meaningful traffic and engagement signal. Either the brand posture is wrong (try the other one) or the channel mix is wrong (try a different mix).
- **No-go:** zero strangers ordering and no traffic or engagement signal. Stop. Salvage the LLC, return as much capital as possible, regroup.
